# Berlin: When the rent cap hit a constitutional wall, the fight moved from prices to ownership

**1. The system at a glance**

About 85% of Berliners rent (Wetzstein, 2019). Germany made renting secure for life with open-ended leases but let its social housing expire: subsidized units leave the system once loans are repaid, so social rentals fell from 2.09 million in 2006 to 1.05 million in 2024 (URBS 370, 2026). Berlin then tried tools Canada treats as unthinkable. Its *Mietendeckel* froze rents on about 1.5 million homes in 2020. The Federal Constitutional Court voided it in 2021, not because rent caps are illegitimate but because federal law already regulates rents "conclusively" (BVerfG, 2021; Holm, 2021). Campaigners then turned to ownership under Article 15 of the Basic Law. Socialization is **not** in force. In 2021, 59.1% of voters backed a non-binding referendum covering about 240,000 homes (Landeswahlleiterin für Berlin, 2021). A March 2026 framework law takes effect in 2028 but transfers nothing (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**2. What holds it together**

The base is a renter majority that votes: tenants outvote owners (URBS 370, 2026), which is how the referendum won. *Legal:* Article 15 has never been used and requires an act of parliament, proportionality and compensation (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). *Institutional:* private firms owning over 3,000 Berlin flats (cooperatives exempt) would transfer them to a public-law institution governed with tenants and staff. Compensation would be "significantly below market value," which a 2023 expert commission found constitutional (Drohsel, 2023; Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). *Political:* after the Left's September 2026 election win, its coalition-talks paper with the SPD and Greens raised the threshold to 10,000 units and exempted post-2021 buildings. It also capped compensation at €16 billion and promised a bill by mid-2028 (Mietervereinigung Berlin, 2026). The weak point is authority: the federal government may legislate to block it, and the Constitutional Court will likely decide (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**3. Who benefits, who bears the costs**

Wetzstein (2019, p. 280) finds that Berlin's winners are early owners and protected tenants. Its losers are refugees, people "in the middle" and homeless people. Sitting tenants would gain security and a say in management; shareholders would lose returns. Taxpayers would carry the compensation bill, illustratively €40 billion, in a city already dependent on fiscal transfers (Kraemer, 2026). Yet socialization adds no homes, and critics say it deters investment (Kraemer, 2026), so Wetzstein's outsiders gain little. The 2026 compromise sharpens this trade-off: higher thresholds and new-build exemptions cut cost and legal risk but cover fewer tenants. Tenants also bear legal risk: when the Mietendeckel fell, they suddenly owed back rent (Holm, 2021).

**4. Could it work here, and should it?**

*Feasibility:* the legal picture is reversed here. Tenancy is provincial and the Charter does not protect property; Québec already regulates rent increases through the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL, 2025). But the Civil Code (art. 952) and Québec's 2023 expropriation reform require market-value compensation (Gouvernement du Québec, 2023). A below-market transfer would need an explicit act of the National Assembly. Montréal's fragmented plex ownership means size thresholds would catch few units.

*Political barriers:* 66.5% of Canadian households own, against 47% in Germany. Ottawa stopped funding new social housing in 1993, and social housing is now 3.5% of dwellings, half the OECD average (URBS 370, 2026). Montréal has a renter majority, but Québec has no binding citizen-initiated referendum, and the city depends on Québec for powers and money.

*Desirability:* Montréal already runs a market-price version. Using its right of first refusal, it bought about 30 buildings with over 700 flats in Côte-des-Neiges for $103.8 million; the OMHM now manages them (Ville de Montréal, 2024). It is worth scaling, but without new building it only reshuffles scarcity.

**Conclusion: requires major reform.** Buying at market price is possible with existing tools. Reaching Montréal's 20% non-market goal needs stable provincial and federal funding, and below-market socialization is currently unlikely. Berlin shows that private ownership of rental housing is a choice, not a necessity.
