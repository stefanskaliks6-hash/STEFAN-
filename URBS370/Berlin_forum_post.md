# Berlin: When the rent cap hit a constitutional wall, the fight moved from prices to ownership

**1. The system at a glance**

Most Berliners rent, increasingly from large corporate landlords. In 2020 Berlin's *Mietendeckel* froze rents and ordered cuts to excessive ones. In April 2021 the Federal Constitutional Court voided it, not because rent caps are illegitimate but because federal law already regulates rents "conclusively" (Holm, 2021). Campaigners turned from prices to ownership under Article 15 of the Basic Law. Socialization is **not** policy in force. A majority backed the non-binding 2021 referendum. A March 2026 framework law takes effect in 2028 but transfers nothing, and no bill exists (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**2. What holds it together**

*Political:* its base is the tenant campaign Deutsche Wohnen & Co. enteignen, which turned renters into a voting majority. *Institutional:* current proposals target private companies holding more than 3,000 Berlin flats, which could keep 3,000 (cooperatives and non-profits exempt). A public-law institution would hold the stock, with compensation "significantly below market value" (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). *Legal:* Article 15 has never been used. Any transfer needs its own proportionate act of parliament that sets compensation and shows milder limits would not suffice. The weak point is authority. In July 2026 the federal government said it may legislate to block socialization by the Länder; the Constitutional Court will likely decide (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**3. Who benefits, who bears the costs**

Sitting tenants of targeted companies would gain stable rents and a voice in management. Shareholders would lose expected returns, and Berlin taxpayers would fund the payout (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). Tenants of smaller landlords and newcomers are left out, since socialization adds no homes. Critics say subsidized construction or stricter rent rules would be less intrusive (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

The central contradiction is compensation. A lower price lets Berlin buy more and keep rents lower, but raises the risk that courts strike the law down. That trap sank the Mietendeckel, and tenants bore the cost through sudden back-rent claims (Holm, 2021).

**4. Could it work here, and should it?**

*Feasibility:* Berlin's barrier is absent here. Hulchanski (2006) finds "no legal or constitutional impediment to federal or provincial governments engaging in any variety of housing policies" (p. 232). Provinces control property and tenancy (Pomeroy, 2021, pp. 4, 13); Québec already regulates rents through complaint-based arbitration at the Tribunal administratif du logement (p. 14). Compensation is the real limit: Québec's Civil Code (art. 952) requires a just indemnity, so below-market transfer would need a new provincial law.

*Political barriers:* 68% of Canadian homes are owner-occupied and under 5% are non-market (Pomeroy, 2021, p. 1). Hulchanski calls this a dual system that privileges ownership, while households in its "secondary" part, mostly renters, "have little political clout" (p. 240). Quoting Krueckeberg, he asks: "Where are the institutions that promote and protect the economic and political interests of renters?" (p. 228). Berlin built one; Montréal has not.

*Desirability:* Canada faces Berlin's problem. Between 2011 and 2016 it lost over 320,000 rentals under $750, mostly because funds and REITs bought them to raise rents. Pomeroy (2021, p. 39) proposes letting non-profits buy such buildings. Montréal already does this at market price: through its right of first refusal, it bought about 30 buildings with 700+ flats in Côte-des-Neiges for $103.8 million, now run by the OMHM (Ville de Montréal, 2024). This is desirable, but without new building it only shifts who owns scarce housing.

**Conclusion: requires major reform.** Market-price acquisition is possible with existing tools, but scaling it needs dedicated acquisition funding that federal programs have lacked (Pomeroy, 2021, p. 40). Below-market socialization is unlikely for now. Hulchanski warns that our housing system "tends to be taken for granted" (p. 222); Berlin shows that private ownership of rental housing is a choice, not a necessity.
