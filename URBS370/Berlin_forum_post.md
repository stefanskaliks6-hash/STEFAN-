# Berlin and the Move From Rent Caps to Socializing Big Landlords

**1. The system at a glance**

I decided to look at Berlin, and specifically its push to socialize large housing companies after its rent cap failed. In 2020, the *Mietendeckel* froze rents, but in April 2021 the Federal Constitutional Court struck it down. The reason was not that rent caps are wrong, but that federal law already regulates rents "conclusively" (Holm, 2021). Meanwhile, the tenant campaign Deutsche Wohnen & Co. enteignen turned to Article 15 of the Basic Law, which allows socialization. Socialization is not in force, though: a majority backed a non-binding 2021 referendum, and a March 2026 framework law takes effect in 2028 but transfers nothing (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**2. What holds it together**

The proposal targets private companies with more than 3,000 Berlin flats, exempting cooperatives and non-profits, and would move the housing to a public-law institution, with compensation "significantly below market value" (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). What really holds this together is political: the campaign turned renters into a voting majority. Legally, though, it is fragile. Article 15 has never been used, so any transfer needs its own proportionate law with compensation. The federal government has also said it may legislate to block it, so the Constitutional Court will likely decide (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026).

**3. Who benefits, who bears the costs**

If we look at who benefits, it is mainly sitting tenants of targeted companies, who would get stable rents and a say in management. The costs fall on shareholders, who lose returns, and taxpayers, who fund the compensation (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). Tenants of smaller landlords and newcomers are left out, since socialization builds no new homes; critics say subsidized construction would be less intrusive (Schüffner & Dauke, 2026). The main contradiction is compensation: the lower the price, the more housing Berlin can buy, but the higher the risk that courts strike the law down. That is what happened with the rent cap, and tenants paid when landlords demanded back rent (Holm, 2021).

**4. Could it work here, and should it?**

If we keep in mind that Berlin's barrier was legal, it would be more feasible here. Hulchanski (2006) notes there is "no legal or constitutional impediment to federal or provincial governments engaging in any variety of housing policies" (p. 232). Provinces control property and tenancy, and Québec already regulates rents through the Tribunal administratif du logement (Pomeroy, 2021). The real limit is compensation, since Québec's Civil Code (art. 952) requires a just indemnity, so paying below market needs a new law.

The political barriers are bigger: 68% of Canadian homes are owner-occupied and less than 5% are non-market (Pomeroy, 2021). Hulchanski (2006) calls this a dual system that privileges owners, where households in the "secondary" part, mostly renters, "have little political clout" (p. 240). Berlin's tenants organized at a scale that Montréal's have not.

Is it desirable? Canada has Berlin's problem. Between 2011 and 2016 it lost over 320,000 rentals under $750, mostly because funds and REITs bought them to raise rents. Pomeroy (2021) suggests letting non-profits buy them instead. Montréal already does something similar through its right of first refusal, which it used to buy about 30 buildings with more than 700 flats in Côte-des-Neiges for $103.8 million, now managed by the OMHM (Ville de Montréal, 2024). I think this is the better version for us, but without new construction it only changes who owns the housing.

Overall, I would say this requires major reform. Buying at market price is possible with existing tools, but scaling it needs funding that federal programs have not provided (Pomeroy, 2021). Socializing below market value is currently unlikely. Still, Berlin shows that private ownership of rental housing is a choice, not a necessity, even if our system "tends to be taken for granted" (Hulchanski, 2006, p. 222).
