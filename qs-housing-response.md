The promise I chose is Québec solidaire's plan to limit rent increases to the rate of inflation for one year and then reform how permitted rent increases are calculated. The page presents this as the first flagship measure of a seven-part housing plan. According to the party, the freeze would give tenants some relief and better protect them from drastic rent increases (Québec solidaire, n.d.). Rent could still go up during that year, but not faster than inflation. After the first year, the way permitted increases are calculated would be reformed, but the page does not explain how the new calculation would work.

At first, this seemed like a fairly reasonable way to give renters more stability. What stood out to me is how rent increases affect the way people divide their income among necessities. If someone is already struggling after paying rent, an increase above inflation makes it harder to cover food, transportation, and other bills. The people who would benefit most are tenants who would otherwise face increases above inflation, especially students, low-wage workers, and seniors on fixed pensions, whose incomes often do not rise fast enough to keep up. Staying in the same neighbourhood also matters. If people can afford to stay where they are, they are not pushed away from their school, work, or family. My initial impression is that more predictable housing costs could help people keep their current living situation. A cap does not really improve anyone's situation, though; it protects it from getting worse. I also do not assume it would make housing affordable for everyone.

The financial costs would fall mainly on landlords who were planning to raise rents above inflation. For one year, they would collect less income than they expected, because they could not charge the larger increases they had planned. This would not affect every owner the same way. Landlords who were only planning small increases would barely notice the cap, while those facing rising costs for taxes, insurance, or repairs could find it harder to pay for maintaining their buildings. There could also be costs for the government, such as enforcing the cap, but the housing page of Québec solidaire does not mention any. Since the page does not explain the reform that would follow the first year, it is also hard to know who would bear the costs in the long run.

One question I still have is how Québec solidaire would make sure the quality of housing stays the same while protecting tenants from increases they cannot afford. By quality, I mean repairs, maintenance, heating, and keeping buildings safe. This matters to me because a smaller rent increase is not much help if the apartment slowly falls apart. If landlords respond to the cap by delaying repairs, tenants could end up paying less but living in worse conditions, which would undo part of the protection the promise is meant to give.
