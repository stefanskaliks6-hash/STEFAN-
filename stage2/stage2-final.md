# Stage 2 – The consequence: Québec solidaire's inflation cap on rent increases

## The promise and its strongest case

For Stage 2, I am going back to the same promise from Stage 1: Québec solidaire's (QS) plan to limit rent increases to the rate of inflation during the first year of a QS government (Québec solidaire, n.d.-a). The full housing plan goes further. Within 100 days, QS would make annual increases follow inflation but never go above 3%, both when a lease is renewed and when a new lease is signed. It would also create a national rent registry so a new tenant can see what the last tenant paid (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). On October 5, QS won 9 of the 127 seats and the Parti québécois formed a minority government (Assemblée nationale du Québec, 2026), so the promise won't happen the way it was written. It still shows who the party wants to protect first. My argument is that the cap is a fair protection for tenants who already have a lease, but on its own it moves part of the cost onto people who are still looking for one.

I think the strongest case for the promise starts with how QS sees housing: as a right and not a speculative asset (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). Madden and Marcuse (2016) explain the housing crisis as commodification, where "a structure's function as real estate takes precedence over its usefulness as a place to live" (Chapter 1). They also name rent control as a form of "partial decommodification" that lets low-income tenants keep a foothold in neighbourhoods where rents are going up (Madden & Marcuse, 2016, Chapter 1). What stood out to me is their point that a home's value comes from its place in a community, which needs continuity and stability. That was my own reason in Stage 1 for why staying in a neighbourhood matters. A cap doesn't take property away from anyone, but it limits how fast someone's home can be turned into income.

The promise also makes practical sense. Quebec has a yearly recommended increase from the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL), but it is only a guide. If a landlord asks for more, it is the tenant who has to refuse and risk a hearing, and a lot of tenants just accept it. QS's design flips this, so the landlord would have to justify going above the limit. Rents could still go up with inflation, so landlords keep their real income. Ontario shows what happens without this protection. There, the guideline only covers sitting tenants, and landlords can reset the rent between tenants. Between 2014 and 2023, the guideline allowed 16.5% in total increases, but average rents went up about 54.5% (Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). QS's cap at signing and the registry are meant to close that gap.

## Who gains, who loses, and what the math builds

From how I see it, the winners are a smaller group than just "tenants." Where landlords already follow the TAL guideline, not much changes. The people who gain are tenants who would have gotten a big increase and accepted it, like the low-wage workers and seniors on fixed pensions I named in Stage 1. For example, on a $1,200 rent, a 3% increase is $36 a month and a 6% increase is $72, so the cap would save that household about $430 a year. That matters to someone already struggling, but it lowers no one's rent.

The losers are uneven too. A small landlord facing higher taxes or repair costs would have to go to the TAL and wait. On paper, financialized landlords lose the most. August (2020) shows that real estate investment trusts went from owning no Canadian apartment suites in 1996 to 164,498 in 2017. Their strategy is to buy older buildings and "reposition" them so rents go up when tenants leave, and August links this to Ontario removing rent control between tenants in the late 1990s. A cap at signing with a registry would cut right into that model. But if only the popular first step happened, covering renewals and not new leases, Quebec would end up with something like the Ontario system. Turnover would pay, and there would be more pressure to push long-term tenants out (August, 2020; Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). The cost would then fall on the next tenant, not the landlord.

When it comes to the math, the cap doesn't build anything; it controls existing leases. The supply side of the plan is 50,000 social, community and non-speculative units in a first mandate, but only 25,000 would be newly built. The other 25,000 would be existing buildings taken off the private market (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). Garcia et al. (2023) explain that any project "must demonstrate the ability to meet an acceptable financial return in order to obtain the capital" it needs (p. 3). In their California case studies, the rents needed to make new buildings work were only affordable to the highest-income households (p. 14). So housing for low-income people needs public money. Buying a building protects the people already there, but it adds no home for someone without one. Madden and Marcuse (2016) argue that cities need more new decommodified housing, like public or cooperative homes. By that test, only half of QS's 50,000 units actually add homes, and the people with the least to offer a pro forma are the ones most likely to keep waiting.

The math also explains the main argument against the cap. Ontario exempted units first occupied after November 2018 from its guideline, saying rent control discourages construction, although Tranjan and Vargatoth (2024) question that argument. In my opinion, the plan should have addressed this for Quebec.

## Whose housing need becomes invisible

The promise tells a simple story: the problem is abusive increases, and the solution is a cap and a registry (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). Madden and Marcuse (2016) question this story. Landlord groups say only a few "bad apples" break the rules, but buying an "underperforming" building and repositioning it to raise the rent happens every day, because it follows from the logic of a commodified system and not just individual greed. The story also only sees one kind of person, someone who already has a lease and wants to keep it. It measures need by the increase, not by the rent level, income, or having no home at all.

That leaves a lot of people out. Young people leaving home, students who move every year or two, newcomers, people leaving violence and evicted households all face the asking rent of an empty unit, and that unit goes to whoever can pay. As Madden and Marcuse (2016) put it, "ability to pay is unequal while the need for a place to live is universal" (Chapter 1). The cap at signing would only help these groups if the registry is actually built and enforced. Tenants who already pay more than 30% of their income on rent, the affordability line Garcia et al. (2023) also use, are protected from it getting worse, but the cap locks in a rent they already can't afford.

People experiencing homelessness are the most invisible, because a cap on increases does nothing for someone who has no rent to cap. Thistle (2017) goes further and defines Indigenous homelessness not as lacking a structure but through Indigenous worldviews, including being cut off from land, relationships and culture. A promise built around leases can't see that kind of need. For them, only new non-market housing made with them really matters.

## Reflection: what my Stage 1 missed

My Stage 1 missed four things. First, I treated a limit at inflation as something new, when Quebec already has a TAL guideline. The real change is who has to enforce it. Second, I cited QS's housing plan but didn't notice that it extends the cap to new leases. I said the "mandatory online form calculating rent increase" was a good idea without really knowing why. After reading August (2020) and Tranjan and Vargatoth (2024), I understand that a registry is what stops the cost from moving to the next tenant.

Third, I treated tenants as one group. I listed students as main winners, but every time a student moves, they sign a new lease at the asking rent. As a student renter, I am closer to the outsider this promise forgets than to the insider it protects. Fourth, my question was about quality, because I live in an older place. I still don't have a full answer, and I still think, like I wrote in Stage 1, that it "doesn't help if the apartment falls apart."

In Stage 1, I also wrote that a limit "doesn't improve anyone's situation from how I see it but it helps protect those from it getting worse." I still agree with that. What changed is how I see who pays. In Stage 1, I thought landlords would carry the cost. Now I see that without the registry and the cap at signing, part of it falls on whoever rents next and on the people the promise never counted. In my opinion, the cap is only fair if it comes with those protections and with social housing that actually gets built.
