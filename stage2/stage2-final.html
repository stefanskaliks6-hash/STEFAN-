Stage 2 – The consequence: Québec solidaire’s inflation cap on rent increases

The promise and its strongest case

For Stage 2, I am going back to the same promise from Stage 1: Québec solidaire’s (QS) plan to limit rent increases to the rate of inflation for one year (Québec solidaire, n.d.-a). The full housing plan goes further. Yearly increases would follow inflation but never go above 3%, both at renewal and when a new lease is signed. A mandatory online form would calculate each fair increase, and landlords could only go higher for improvement work, with the costs shown to tenants. A national rent registry would let a new tenant see what the last tenant paid (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 6). On October 5, QS won 9 of the 127 seats and the Parti québécois formed a minority government (Assemblée nationale du Québec, 2026), so the promise won’t happen as written. My argument is that the cap is a fair protection for tenants who already have a lease, but on its own it moves part of the cost onto people who are still looking for one.

I think the strongest case starts with how QS sees housing. The plan says the real problem is that housing has been treated as a financial asset instead of a basic need, and it calls for a society where housing becomes “a right rather than a commodity” (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 3, my translation). This is very close to Madden and Marcuse (2016), who describe commodification as when “a structure’s function as real estate takes precedence over its usefulness as a place to live” (Chapter 1). They name rent control as a form of “partial decommodification” that lets low-income tenants keep a foothold in neighbourhoods where rents are going up. What stood out to me is their point that a home’s value comes from its place in a community. That was my own reason in Stage 1 for why staying in a neighbourhood matters.

Quebec has a yearly recommended increase from the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL), but it is only a guide. If a landlord asks for more, the tenant has to refuse and risk a hearing, and a lot of tenants just accept it. QS’s mandatory form flips this, so the landlord has to stay under the limit unless they can show the cost of real work. The plan also points out that the biggest increases happen when long-term tenants leave (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 6). Ontario shows why that matters. There, the guideline only covers sitting tenants, and landlords can reset the rent between tenants. From 2014 to 2023, the guideline allowed 16.5% in total increases, but average rents went up about 54.5% (Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). August (2020) also found that Quebec, with rent control, has 42% of Canada’s apartments but only 14% of suites owned by real estate investment trusts (REITs), while Ontario holds 42% (p. 984). Manitoba, where control is tied to the unit instead of the tenant, was almost ignored by REITs (p. 983). QS’s cap at signing would work the same way.

Who gains, who loses, and what the math builds

From how I see it, the winners are a smaller group than just “tenants.” Where landlords already follow the TAL guideline, not much changes. The people who gain are tenants who would have gotten a big increase and accepted it, like the low-wage workers and seniors on fixed pensions I named in Stage 1. On a $1,200 rent, a 3% increase is $36 a month and a 6% increase is $72, so the cap would save that household about $430 a year. That matters, but it lowers no one’s rent.

The losers are uneven too. A small landlord facing higher repair costs can still raise the rent for improvement work, but has to show tenants the costs. Financialized landlords lose the most. REITs went from owning no Canadian apartment suites in 1996 to 164,498 in 2017, and their “repositioning” strategy depends on turnover (August, 2020, p. 980). As one CEO put it, “the money and returns are made in the suites when the suites turnover” (p. 986). QS describes the same thing: affordable apartments bought to evict tenants and raise rents to push up resale value (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 3). A cap at signing with a registry cuts right into that model. Still, the registry only lets tenants “better assert their rights” (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 6, my translation). A new tenant has to look up the old rent and act on it, which puts some burden back on them. If only the popular first-year freeze happened, Quebec would end up closer to Ontario after its 1997 Tenant Protection Act, where vacancy decontrol pushed landlords to remove tenants and raise rents (August, 2020, pp. 979–980).

When it comes to the math, the cap doesn’t build anything; it controls existing leases. The supply side is 50,000 social, cooperative and non-profit units in a first mandate, all with rent supplements so no one pays more than 25% of their income. Half would be built over four years, and the other 25,000 would be existing private buildings bought and renovated, because QS says the crisis is about expensive housing and not only supply (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 10). Garcia et al. (2023) explain that a project “must demonstrate the ability to meet an acceptable financial return in order to obtain the capital” it needs (p. 3). In their case studies, the rents needed to make new buildings work were only affordable to the highest-income households (p. 14). QS seems to understand this math. It promises low-interest loans from the Société d’habitation du Québec, a public land reserve, pre-approved designs and group buying of materials to cut project costs by 25% (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 10). Those are the levers in Garcia et al.’s pro forma. But the plan doesn’t say what any of this would cost, and buying a building protects the people already there without adding a home for someone who doesn’t have one.

Whose housing need becomes invisible

QS’s diagnosis is structural, like Madden and Marcuse’s, but its main tool is built around the increase. It sees one kind of person: someone who already has a lease. That leaves people out. Young people leaving home, students and newcomers face the asking rent of an empty unit, which goes to whoever can pay. As Madden and Marcuse (2016) put it, “ability to pay is unequal while the need for a place to live is universal” (Chapter 1). The plan never mentions students or newcomers, so their protection depends on the registry and on them asserting their rights. Tenants who already pay too much in the private market are protected from it getting worse, but the cap locks in a rent they can’t afford. The 25% guarantee only applies in the 50,000 social units.

People experiencing homelessness are not ignored. The plan dedicates part of the new social units to them, with community support (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 13), but it doesn’t say how many. The group that is fully invisible is Indigenous people, who are not mentioned anywhere in the plan. Thistle (2017) writes that Indigenous homelessness “is not defined as lacking a structure of habitation” but as being isolated from relationships to land, family, kin, cultures and identities, and that it comes from colonization, not only the housing market (p. 6). A plan built around leases and units can’t see that kind of need.

Reflection: what my Stage 1 missed

My Stage 1 missed four things. First, I treated a limit at inflation as something new, when Quebec already has a TAL guideline. The real change is who has to enforce it. Second, I said the “mandatory online form calculating rent increase” was a good idea, but that form is in the same section that says the cap applies at lease signing (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 6). I read it but missed what it meant.

Third, I treated tenants as one group. I listed students as main winners, but every time a student moves, they sign a new lease at the asking rent. As a student renter, I am closer to the outsider this promise forgets than to the insider it protects. Fourth, my question was about quality, because I live in an older place. The plan answers more than I expected, with penalties up to expropriation for landlords who neglect their buildings (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b, p. 8). It depends on enforcement, and I still think, like I wrote in Stage 1, that it “doesn’t help if the apartment falls apart.”

In Stage 1, I also wrote that a limit “doesn’t improve anyone’s situation from how I see it but it helps protect those from it getting worse.” I still agree. What changed is how I see who pays. In Stage 1, I thought landlords would pay. Now I see that without the registry and the cap at signing, part of it falls on whoever rents next and on the people the promise never counted. In my opinion, the cap is only fair if it comes with those protections and with social housing that actually gets built.
