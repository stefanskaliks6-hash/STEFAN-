Stage 2 – The consequence: Québec solidaire’s inflation cap on rent increases

The promise and its strongest case

In Stage 1, I chose Québec solidaire’s (QS) promise to limit rent increases to inflation during the first year of a QS government (Québec solidaire, n.d.-a). The full housing plan goes further: within 100 days, QS would make annual increases follow inflation without ever going above 3%. The limit would apply both when a lease is renewed and when a new one is signed. A national rent registry would let a new tenant see what the previous one paid (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). On October 5, QS won 9 of the 127 seats and the Parti québécois formed a minority government (Assemblée nationale du Québec, 2026), so the promise will not be carried out as written. It still shows whom the party would protect first. My argument is that the cap is a fair protection for tenants who already have a lease, but on its own it shifts part of the cost onto the people still looking for one.

The strongest case begins with how QS frames housing: as a right, not a speculative asset (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). Madden and Marcuse (2016) describe the housing crisis as commodification, in which “a structure’s function as real estate takes precedence over its usefulness as a place to live” (Chapter 1). They name rent control as a form of “partial decommodification” that lets low-income tenants keep a foothold in neighbourhoods where rents are rising (Madden & Marcuse, 2016, Chapter 1). They also argue that a home’s use value comes from its place in a community, which needs continuity and stability. This was my own reason in Stage 1 for why staying in a neighbourhood matters. A cap takes no property from anyone, but it limits how fast a tenant’s home can be turned into income.

The case is also practical. Quebec already has a yearly recommended increase set by the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL), but it is only a guide. When a landlord asks for more, it is the tenant who must refuse and risk a hearing, and many accept rather than fight. QS’s design moves that burden: the landlord, not the tenant, would have to justify an increase above the limit. Since rents could still rise with inflation, landlords keep their real income, and the plan leaves room for higher increases after major work (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b) [VERIFY in plan]. The cap costs the government little and can start quickly. Ontario shows what happens without these protections. There, the guideline applies only to sitting tenants, so landlords can reset the rent freely between tenants, and rents on units that change hands rise much faster than the guideline (Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). QS’s cap at signing and registry target that gap.

Who gains, who loses, and what the math builds

The winners are a narrower group than “tenants.” Where landlords already respect the TAL guideline, little changes. The people who gain are tenants who would have received a large increase and accepted it, like the low-wage workers and seniors on fixed pensions I named in Stage 1. For a hypothetical $1,200 rent, a 3% increase is $36 a month and a 6% increase is $72, so the cap would save that household about $430 a year. That matters to a household already short, but it lowers no one’s rent.

The losers are uneven too. A small landlord facing rising taxes, insurance or repair costs would have to apply to the TAL and wait. On paper, financialized landlords lose the most. August (2020) shows that real estate investment trusts went from owning no Canadian apartment suites in 1996 to 164,498 in 2017. Their strategy is to buy older buildings and “reposition” them so that rents rise when tenants leave. August also shows that Ontario’s vacancy decontrol, adopted in 1997, made that strategy profitable. A cap at signing, backed by a registry, would cut directly into this model. If only the popular first step were adopted, covering renewals but not new leases, Quebec would reproduce the Ontario system. Turnover would pay, and the pressure to push long-term tenants out would grow (August, 2020; Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). The cost would then fall on the next tenant, not the landlord.

On the math, the cap builds nothing. It regulates existing tenancies, not the production of new homes. Supply is supposed to come from another part of the plan: 50,000 social, community and non-speculative units over a first mandate. Only about half of these would be newly built; the rest would be existing buildings bought or converted (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b) [VERIFY 25,000 split in plan]. Garcia et al. (2023) show that a project gets built only when its expected rents cover land, construction, financing and the return that lenders and investors require. Housing for low-income households almost never meets that test on its own, so it depends on public money. Yet the plan gives no cost for these units, or for enforcing the cap, which was the question I raised in Stage 1 (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b) [VERIFY no costs in plan]. Buying a building protects the people already in it but adds no home for someone without one. Madden and Marcuse (2016) argue that the real answer is more new decommodified housing, such as public or cooperative homes. By that test, the weakest part of QS’s plan is the part with no budget. The household with the least to offer a pro forma is the one most likely to keep waiting.

The same math explains the strongest objection. Ontario exempted units first occupied after November 2018 from its guideline, arguing that controls discourage construction (Tranjan & Vargatoth, 2024). The plan does not say whether a cap at signing would slow private rental building in Quebec.

Whose housing need becomes invisible

The promise tells a simple story: the problem is the abusive increase, and the solution is the cap. Madden and Marcuse (2016) question this kind of story. Landlord groups blame a few “bad apples,” but buying an “underperforming” building and repositioning it to raise the rent happens every day. It follows from the logic of a commodified system, not from individual greed. The story also sees only one kind of person: someone who already has a lease and wants to keep it. Its unit of need is the increase, not the rent level, the household’s income, or the lack of a home.

That leaves out several groups. Young people leaving home, students who move every year or two, newcomers, people leaving violence and evicted households all face the asking rent of a vacant unit, and a vacant unit goes to whoever can pay. As Madden and Marcuse (2016) put it, “ability to pay is unequal while the need for a place to live is universal” (Chapter 1). The cap at signing would help these groups only if the registry is built and enforced. Tenants already paying far more than 30% of their income, the affordability line we used in class, are protected from getting worse, but the cap locks in a rent they cannot afford.

People experiencing homelessness are the most invisible, because a cap on increases does nothing for someone with no rent to cap. Thistle (2017) goes further: Indigenous homelessness is not only the lack of a structure but a loss of connection to land, family, community and culture, a need that a promise built around leases cannot see. For these groups, only new non-market housing designed with them matters, and that is the uncosted part of the plan.

Reflection: what my Stage 1 missed

Stage 1 missed four things. First, I treated a limit at inflation as something new, when Quebec already has a TAL guideline. The real change is who must enforce it. Second, I cited QS’s housing plan but did not notice that it extends the cap to lease signing. I called the “mandatory online form calculating rent increase” a good idea without understanding why. After reading August (2020) and Tranjan and Vargatoth (2024), I see that a registry is what stops the cost from moving to the next tenant.

Third, I treated tenants as one group. I listed students as main winners, but every time a student moves, they sign a new lease at the asking rent. As a student renter, I am closer to the outsider this promise forgets than to the insider it protects. Fourth, my open question was about quality, because I live in an older building. The plan’s answer, the TAL route for major work, has no cost attached. In Stage 1, I wrote that it “doesn’t help if the apartment falls apart,” and I still think that.

I also wrote that a limit “doesn’t improve anyone’s situation from how I see it but it helps protect those from it getting worse.” I still agree. What changed is how I see who pays. In Stage 1, I assumed landlords would carry the cost. Now I see that, without the registry and the cap at signing, part of it falls on whoever rents next and on the people the promise never counted. The cap is fair only if it comes with those protections and with social housing that is actually built and paid for.
