For stage 2 I went back to stage 1, Quebec Solidaire had said that in their first year of government rent increases would be capped at inflation rate (Quebec Solidaire 2026). Following the first year of their housing plan, increases would follow inflation but they would never go over. If landlords wanted more than that, they can only go higher if they do improvement work on the building, and they have to show the tenants what the work cost (Quebec Solidaire, 2026). Quebec solidaire only had won 9 out of 127 seats and the Parti québécois formed a minority government (Assemblée Nationale du Québec, 2026), so when I look at the cap as sort of a design choice not a policy that is being set in place. I think that the cap is a pretty good tool for the tenants that already have a lease, but by itself it bears the cost to those who are actively looking for housing and that is not fair.

I think the way that Quebec solidaire sees and views housing is their strong suit. Housing is a right not an asset or commodity (Quebec Solidaire, 2026). It relates with the reading assigned in class from Madden and Marcuse as it relates to the function of housing. They name rent control as a form of a decommodification that allows low income tenants to keep their homes where the rent is going up and they can’t afford. I think what stood out to me the most is how that a home's value comes from a place in a community, that was my own reason in stage 1 of why having to stay in the same neighborhood matters so much.

The Tribunal Administratif du Logement (TAL), recommends yearly an increase for Quebec, but they don’t have to follow it, it acts as a guide. If a landlord wants more and asks for more from the tenant has to refuse and risk a hearing, and most tenants just accept it and allow the rent to increase. Quebec Solidaire form flips this and the landlord has to stay under the limit unless they can show the cost of real work being done. The plan points out something that caught my attention about the biggest increases happening usually when the long term renting tenants vacate and leave somewhere else (Quebec solidaire). A report from the tenant rights group Regroupement des comités logement et associations de locataires du Québec (RCLALQ) backs this up, since between 2020 and 2024 the asking rents for available units in Montréal went up 27% while inflation was only 17% (RCLALQ, 2024). In one of our class readings, August (2020), found that in Quebec with rent control 42% of Canada’s apartments but 14% is owned by REIT’s. If the Quebec solidaire cap at signing would work the same way.

From how I am looking at it the winners are smaller than just tenants and renters. Where landlords already follow the TAL guideline not a lot changes or shifts. So the people who are gaining are the tenants who would have gotten a larger rent increase and took it and accepted it without doing anything, such as low wage workers and people that are on fixed income as I mentioned in stage 1.

For losses it is uneven too. A smaller landlord that is facing higher repair costs can still raise the rent price for work on the building and fixing things up and make sure that living situations are good, but they have to demonstrate and present the tenants the costs and show the repairs. Overall I think that financialized landlords are at the highest loss, as REITs went from owning zero Canadian apartments in 1996 to owning 164,498 suites in 2017, which is about 10% of the private apartment stock in Canada (August, 2020). Quebec solidaire describes affordable apartments bought to evict tenants and raise rents and to boost up the resale value (Quebec Solidaire, 2026). I think a cap at signing with a registry relates to that, even though the registry only lets the tenants better express their rights. A new tenant has to look at the old rent paid and do something about it which puts some stress back on them.

When it comes to the math, the cap doesn't build anything, it controls existing leases from renters. On the supply side 50,000 are social and cooperative non profit units where no one pays more than a quarter of their income. The other half would be developed over four years, and the rest 25,000, would be existing private housing (Quebec Solidaire, 2026). Garcia et al. (2023) mention that a project has to show the ability to meet an appropriate return on investment in order to get the capital that it needs. I think that in their case, the rents needed to make new buildings would work if only the wealthy households. Quebec solidaire promises low interest loans from the Societe d’habitation du Quebec, and separately a public land reserve, pre-approved building plans and group buying of materials to cut project costs by 25% (Quebec Solidaire). But their plan doesn't mention anything of what this would cost, and purchasing a building helps the people already there without adding a home for someone that doesn't have one.

Quebec solidaires main tool is around the rent increase. They look at the kind of person who has a lease, and that leaves people without one out. Such as students leaving home and having to pay rent for a unit. I like how Madden and Marcuse (2016) say it “ability to pay is unequal while the need for a place to live is universal” which is something that stuck with me. Quebec solidaires plan doesn’t mention students or new migrants, so them being protected and not facing the increase depends on them.

Renters who already pay way too much in the private market are actually protected from their rent getting worse, and that rent cap is something that they can't afford. Those experiencing homelessness are not invisible. Quebec solidaire dedicates an area of the new social units for those without a home, with the help of community support, but they don't mention how many.(Québec Solidaire). A group that is fully ignored is Indigenous people who aren't mentioned in the plan and targets, and their plan does not meet the needs of family, culture, and relationship to land (Thistle 2017).
