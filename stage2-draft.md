# Stage 2 – The consequence: Québec solidaire's inflation cap on rent increases

## The promise and its strongest case

In Stage 1, I chose Québec solidaire's (QS) promise to limit rent increases to inflation in the first year of a QS government (Québec solidaire, n.d.-a). The full housing plan makes this permanent: within 100 days, annual increases would follow inflation but never go above 3%, both when a lease is renewed and when a new one is signed. A national rent registry would let tenants see what the previous occupant paid (Québec solidaire, 2026; n.d.-b). On October 5, QS won 9 of the 127 seats and the Parti québécois formed a minority government (Assemblée nationale du Québec, 2026), so the promise will not be implemented as written. It still shows how the party thinks about who deserves protection. My argument is that the cap is a reasonable protection for tenants who already have a lease, but on its own it moves part of the cost onto people who are still looking for housing.

The strongest case starts with how QS sees housing. For the party, housing is a right and not a speculative asset, and increases of 10, 20 or even 30% come from a minority of abusive landlords (Québec solidaire, 2026). This is the distinction Madden and Marcuse (2016) make between housing's use value as a home and its exchange value as an investment. When exchange value wins, the people who live in a building carry the risk of someone else's profit strategy. The cap tries to protect use value.

The case also rests on a real gap in Quebec's system. Since 2026, the Tribunal administratif du logement (TAL) calculates a recommended increase from a three-year average of inflation, which gave 3.1% for 2026 (Tribunal administratif du logement, 2026). But that rate is only a guide. If a landlord asks for more, the tenant has to refuse and risk a hearing, and few do [VERIFY in Léger (2026): 8% have ever had their rent set by the TAL; 28% would accept an above-guideline increase]. CMHC (2025) found that Montréal rents rose 7.2% in 2025, mostly through lease renewals, even as vacancy rose. Using the mechanisms from class (Ghaffari, 2026a), the cap changes who is accountable. The landlord, not the tenant, would have to justify going above the limit. Rents still follow inflation, so landlords do not lose real income, and the plan says owners who do major work could still ask the TAL for more (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). The goal of letting people stay in their homes is supported by research: in San Francisco, rent control made covered tenants about 20% more likely to stay at their address (Diamond et al., 2019).

## Who gains, who loses, and what the math builds

The winners are a smaller group than "tenants." Where landlords already respect the TAL rate, the cap changes little. The people who gain are tenants who would have received a higher increase and not fought it, like the low-wage workers and seniors on fixed pensions I named in Stage 1. On an average rent of $1,170 [VERIFY in Léger, 2026], a 3.1% increase is about $36 a month and a 6% increase about $70, so the cap would save that household around $400 a year. That matters to a household that is already short, but it does not lower anyone's rent.

The losers are uneven too. A small landlord facing a real jump in taxes, insurance or repairs would have to apply to the TAL and wait. On paper, financialized landlords lose the most. August (2020) shows that real estate investment trusts went from owning no Canadian apartment suites in 1996 to 164,498 in 2017. Their business model depends on "repositioning" buildings, which means raising rents when units turn over. These landlords also have the most ways out. In San Francisco, landlords facing rent control reduced rental supply by 15% by converting and redeveloping, which pushed citywide rents up 5.1% (Diamond et al., 2019). Kholodilin's (2024) review finds that rent control does slow rent growth in controlled units. But in 10 of the 12 studies that measured it, rents were higher in uncontrolled housing, and the studies almost all find lower housing quality.

The weak point is when a unit changes tenants. Between 2020 and 2024, asking rents for available units in Montréal rose 27% while inflation was 17% (Regroupement des comités logement et associations de locataires du Québec [RCLALQ], 2024). Clause G of the lease, which is supposed to tell a new tenant the previous rent, does not stop this, because new tenants rarely know what was paid before. This is the information asymmetry we discussed in class (Ghaffari, 2026a). If only the first-year cap happened, covering renewals but not new leases, it would work like the vacancy decontrol Ontario adopted in 1997 (August, 2020). Turnover would become more profitable, and the cost would move to the next tenant. The full plan answers this with the cap at signing and the registry (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b). So who wins depends on whether the whole package is implemented or only the popular first step.

On the math, the cap builds nothing. In course terms, it regulates tenure, not production (Ghaffari, 2026b). It moves money between private parties and protects homes that already exist. Supply is supposed to come from 50,000 social, community and non-speculative units over four years. But only about half would be newly built; the rest would be existing buildings bought or converted (Québec solidaire, n.d.-b) [VERIFY the 25,000 split in the plan]. Buying a building protects the people already living there, but it does not add a home for someone without one. The plan also gave no cost for these measures at launch, including the enforcement I wondered about in Stage 1 (Québec solidaire, 2026). The math that would decide whose housing gets built is missing.

## Whose housing need becomes invisible

The promise rests on a causal story (Ghaffari, 2026b): the culprit is the abusive landlord, the mechanism is the uncontrolled increase, and the tool is the cap (Québec solidaire, 2026). That story sees one kind of person: someone who already has a lease and wants to keep it.

It does not see people who are searching. Young people leaving home, students who move every year or two, newcomers, people leaving violence and evicted households all pay the asking rent, and the RCLALQ (2024) numbers show that is where rents rise fastest. CMHC (2025) also links Montréal's rising vacancy mainly to fewer temporary residents, such as international students and temporary workers. They are counted in the vacancy rate but are not voters in a Quebec election, and they appear nowhere in the promise. People experiencing homelessness are the most invisible, because a cap on increases does nothing for someone who has no rent to cap. For them only new non-market housing matters, and that is the uncosted part of the plan.

The promise also hides tenants whose rent is already too high. One in five Quebec tenants (21%) has trouble paying rent (Léger, 2026). A cap at inflation protects them from getting worse, but it also locks in a rent they already cannot afford.

## Reflection: what my Stage 1 missed

Stage 1 missed four things. First, I treated a limit at inflation as something new. Since 2026, the TAL's own rate already follows inflation, so the real change is who has to do the work of enforcing it. Second, I cited QS's full housing plan in Stage 1 but did not notice that it extends the cap to lease signing. I did mention the "mandatory online form calculating rent increase" as a good idea, but I did not see that a registry is what makes the cap work when a unit changes tenants.

Third, I treated tenants as one group. I listed students as main winners, but students move often, and every move is a new lease at the asking rent. As a student renter, I am closer to the outsider this promise forgets than to the insider it protects. Fourth, my open question was about quality, because I live in an older building. That worry was fair, since the research almost always finds lower quality under rent control (Kholodilin, 2024). The plan's answer is the TAL route for major work, but it has no cost attached. In Stage 1, I wrote that the cap "doesn't help if the apartment falls apart," and I still think that.

I also wrote in Stage 1 that a limit "doesn't improve anyone's situation from how I see it but it helps protect those from it getting worse." I still agree. What changed is that I now see the first-year cap as a transfer to people who already have housing, paid partly by landlords and possibly by whoever rents next. In my opinion, it is only fair if it comes with the registry, the cap at signing, and social housing that is actually built and costed.
